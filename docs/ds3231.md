# How the firmware talks to the DS3231

This is the spike for issue 56, and what I built from it (the last section). I wanted to know what the firmware (v7.00.00b5) does with the
board's DS3231 so that I can model a chip that gives it the host's time. The decision is already
made: the STM32's own RTC stays on emulated time, and the DS3231 model supplies the host's real
time, with writes setting an offset.

The short answer, in the same spirit as the SD card one:

**The firmware does not use an I2C controller for the DS3231. It bit-bangs two GPIO pins, PD4
(SDA) and PD5 (SCL), with the HAL's GPIO read and write calls and microsecond delays.** The three
hardware controllers that the firmware does initialise (I2C1, I2C2, I2C4) never move a byte for the
chip. So the smallest sufficient model is an I2C slave watching two GPIO pins, and no I2C controller
model at all. It is the same shape as the SD card slot.

Two things in the issue text do not match this firmware, so I checked them rather than assumed:

- There is no `OPTION RTC AUTO ENABLE`, `RTC GETTIME`, `RTC SETTIME`, `RTC GETREG` or `RTC SETREG`.
  The only `RTC` words in the firmware's strings are `OPTION RTC CALIBRATE`, `OPTION RTC DRIVE`,
  `RTC hardware error` and `RTC not responding`. The way to switch the chip on is `OPTION DS3231 ON`
  (manual, High Accuracy Real Time Clock and the OPTION list), which sets a saved option and reboots.
  `MM.INFO(RTC)` is 1 when it is on. Observed at the prompt: `OPTION RTC AUTO ENABLE` gives
  `Unrecognised option`, `RTC SETTIME 26,11,14,12,0,0` gives `Unknown command`, and `RTC GETTIME`
  prints an empty line.
- The chip is used through the ordinary `TIME$` and `DATE$`, as the manual says, not through RTC
  commands. I2C commands from a program (`I2C OPEN`, `I2C WRITE`) are a different thing: they use the
  hardware controllers and the rear connector, and cannot reach this chip.

## How I found this

- Schematic (`reference/construction-pack/g2/Schematic.pdf`, read as an image at 600 dpi) and the BOM
  (`JLCBOMV2.1.xlsx`, which lists `DS3231MZ+TRL`, IC7, as an optional part).
- The manual (`pdftotext`): High Accuracy Real Time Clock, Appendix B (I2C), OPTION DS3231, TIME$ and
  DATE$.
- The real firmware, run to the prompt from a flash file that has been through the first boot, with
  `--trace-mmio`, with and without `OPTION DS3231 ON`. Traces were taken as streams filtered while
  they ran, and windows of 10 to 30 thousand instructions with `--trace-from`; nothing large was kept.
- A linear Thumb-2 disassembly of the `.bin`, read by me (no source was used or copied). Every
  address below is from it.
- To see the whole conversation I built a scratch copy of the repo outside the tree, with a
  throwaway DS3231 slave of about 150 lines on PD4 and PD5 and a hook in the GPIO port D. I ran the real
  firmware against it. It was deleted with the scratch copy and nothing of it is in the repository.
  "Observed" below means seen in those runs (or in the runs with no chip, which need no scratch code),
  "read" means from the disassembly, and "guessed" is what it says.

## 1. Pins and controller

From the schematic (observed, drawing): DS3231MZ+ (IC7) pin 7 SDA goes to STM32 pin 146, **PD4**, and
pin 8 SCL goes to pin 147, **PD5**. Each wire has a 10K pull-up to 3V3 (R70 and R71), so the bus
idles high with nothing driving it. Pin 6 (VBAT) is on the VBAT net, which is the coin cell (CR1220 in
the construction guide). Pins 1 (32KHZ), 3 (INT/SQW) and 4 (RST) have no wire on the drawing, so there
is no alarm interrupt to model. PD4 and PD5 have no I2C function on the STM32H743, which is why it is
bit-banged.

The rear connector's I2C pins are separate nets (PB8 I2C1_SCK, PB9 I2C1_SDA, PB7 I2C2_SDA, with 10K
pull-ups R38 to R40 and R52), and the front panel's nunchuk pins are PH4 and PB11. None of them is the
DS3231.

What the firmware does with the pins (observed, `GPIOD_*` accesses with the option on):

- Setup: `GPIOD_MODER` goes from 0xAFEAEF1A to 0xAFEAE71A and 0xAFEAE51A (PD5, then PD4, as outputs),
  and ends at 0xAFEAE41A when PD4 is turned into an input to read an acknowledge. `GPIOD_PUPDR` and
  `GPIOD_OTYPER` are rewritten with the same values. The pin set-up function is the one at 0x0802B280
  (called with pin 0x10 or 0x20 and a mode flag), which goes through the generic HAL pin code at
  0x08004316 to 0x08004328.
- Each level change is one write to `GPIOD_BSRR` from 0x0800457A (the HAL write-pin function at
  0x08004578): 0x10 sets SDA, 0x20 sets SCL, 0x100000 clears SDA, 0x200000 clears SCL. Reads of SDA
  are `GPIOD_IDR` reads from 0x0800456C (the HAL read-pin function). No `GPIOD_ODR` write.
- The pins are push-pull outputs, never open drain, so SCL is never released and the firmware does not
  support clock stretching. SDA is an output while the firmware sends and an input while it listens.
- Delays are the firmware's microsecond routine at 0x0809ADC8 (called with 1) between every edge, and
  its millisecond routine at 0x0809ADE0 in the acknowledge wait. They are made of `TIM16_CNT` reads, which is why
  `TIM16_CNT` dominates a trace while a transaction is in progress (43 million reads in the first
  600 million instructions with no chip).

The hardware controllers: I2C1 (0x40005400), I2C2 (0x40005800) and I2C4 (0x58001C00) are initialised
by the HAL early in boot (instruction 1,284,326 on, 32 accesses each: `CR1`, `CR2`, `OAR1`, `OAR2` and a
`TIMINGR` of 0x10707DBC, the same value for all three) and de-initialised after the banner
(instruction 412,061,912 to 412,064,960), with their pins moved around. There is no access to `ISR`,
`TXDR`, `RXDR` or `ICR`, and no START bit set in `CR2`, in any run. I2C3 (0x40005C00) is never touched.
No interrupt, no DMA: everything is polling.

## 2. What happens today

With the flash from first boot (`--on "Keyboard type" "1" --on "HH:MM:SS" "12:00:00\r" --on
"DD/MM/YY" "12/11/26\r"`), without the option, the clock is the STM32's and there is no DS3231 traffic
at all (observed: no PD4 or PD5 activity in a run to the prompt and a `?TIME$:?DATE$`). `?TIME$` gives
12:00:02 (the saved 12:00:00 plus emulated time) and `MM.INFO(RTC)` is 0.

`OPTION DS3231 ON` at the prompt prints nothing and the system resets (the emulator ends the run at
the reset: `cmm2-emulator: system reset`). Starting again on the same flash file:

- Nothing answers on PD4, so the firmware sees a missing chip. **It does not hang and prints no
  error.** `RTC not responding` is in the firmware but I did not see it on any DS3231 path.
- Every byte it sends waits for an acknowledge: SDA is read after the ninth clock, and if it is still
  high the wait loop (0x08010724) polls up to 251 times with a 1 ms delay (the counter is the
  constant 0xFB in the code; I counted 75,277,358 instructions for one timeout, about 0.34 s of machine
  time at the clock model's 2.2 cycles an instruction). After 251 failures it sends a STOP and returns
  1. The caller ignores that for the address and register bytes, so a missing chip costs three timeouts
  for each read (address 0xD0, register, address 0xD1) and the bytes it then reads are all 0xFF
  (SDA high, pulled up): seven reads of 0xFF, with the master acknowledging all but the last.
- The banner appears at about 412.06 million instructions as before, but the prompt (`> `) only at
  about 1.3 to 1.4 billion (observed between 1.3G and 1.4G), against about 412 million with the chip
  there: four time reads at boot, each three timeouts. Each command typed at the prompt then costs
  a second or two of the same waiting (see section 3).
- The 0xFF bytes are converted as if they were BCD: `TIME$` prints `45:85:85` and `DATE$` prints
  `45-25-2165`, and the firmware writes that garbage into the STM32's RTC at boot (I read back the
  saved calendar: TR 0x00050511, DR 0x0025F205, from the `--flash` file after the run). `TIME$ =
  "10:00:00"` is accepted and has no effect, because the write is not acknowledged either and the next
  `TIME$` read gets 0xFF again.

So a model has to be there when the option is on, or the machine is slow and shows nonsense. That is
reason enough for the model to exist, apart from the host time.

An older bug showed up on the way, and is worth a test. The firmware decodes the month with a one-bit
`sbfx` (bit 4, the ten of October to December) and a mask. The copy of the repo I started from (taken
2026-11-13) had `SBFX` sign-extending from the wrong bit when the field does not start at bit 0, so
November printed as `01` and December as `02`, and the STM32 RTC got the same wrong month. I
confirmed that with a unit test in the scratch copy. The live tree's `exec_sbfx` in
`crates/zmu_cortex_m/src/executor/misc_data_processing.rs` already sign-extends from `widthminus1`
(read, not run), so it is fixed there; the DS3231 tests below should include a date in October to
December so that it stays fixed.

## 3. The transactions

The address bytes are 0xD0 (write) and 0xD1 (read) in the code, so the 7-bit address is 0x68. The
primitives, all read from the disassembly:

| Address | What |
|---|---|
| 0x080105F8 | START: SDA high, SCL high, SDA low, SCL low |
| 0x08010650 | STOP: SDA low, SCL high, SDA high |
| 0x08010698 | write one byte, MSB first (SCL low, set SDA, SCL high, SCL low, 8 times), then SDA released |
| 0x08010724 | wait for the acknowledge: SDA input, SCL high, poll SDA; low is ACK |
| 0x08010820 | read one byte; the argument says whether to acknowledge it (0x08010788, SDA low) or not (0x080107D8) |
| 0x0801088C | `ds_write(reg, buffer, length)`: START, 0xD0, reg, the bytes, STOP |
| 0x080108E0 | `ds_read(reg, buffer, length)`: START, 0xD0, reg, STOP, START, 0xD1, the bytes, STOP |

Note that the read is a STOP and a new START between the register write and the read address, not a
repeated START. A slave has to accept that (a real DS3231 does: the pointer stays where it was left).
The last byte of a read is not acknowledged.

The commands (call sites read from the disassembly, bytes seen in the scratch runs):

| What | Call site | Transaction |
|---|---|---|
| Sync the STM32 RTC from the chip (function at 0x08069FC0) | 0x08069FDA | read 7 bytes from register 0 |
| `TIME$` read | 0x0806AAB2 | read 3 bytes from register 0 (seconds, minutes, hours) |
| `DATE$` read | 0x0806A48E | read 3 bytes from register 4 (date, month, year) |
| `TIME$ = "HH:MM:SS"` | 0x0806A970 | write 3 bytes at register 0 |
| `DATE$ = "DD/MM/YY"` | 0x0806A35C | write 4 bytes at register 3 (weekday, date, month, year) |
| `TIME$ = sec` | 0x0806A8E4 then 0x0806A970 | the date write (registers 3 to 6), then the time write (0 to 2) |

Observed in the scratch runs: those, and nothing else. The only registers ever addressed are 0 and 3
and 4. **The control and status registers (0x0E, 0x0F), the aging offset (0x10), the temperature
(0x11, 0x12) and the alarms (0x07 to 0x0D) are never touched** by this firmware: there are only these six call sites of
the read and write routines (0x0801088C and 0x080108E0), none with another register number, and the
start, stop, byte-write and acknowledge primitives are called from nowhere but those two routines
(checked by searching the disassembly for calls to them).

How the bytes are converted (read from the code, and the output checked against it):

- Seconds and minutes: tens digit from bits 6:4 (three bits), units from bits 3:0. The oscillator-stop
  and other flags are not looked at.
- Hours: tens from bits 5:4, units from bits 3:0. **Bit 6 (12/24 hour) is ignored**, so the firmware
  only works with the chip in 24-hour mode. It writes hours with bit 6 clear, so it keeps the chip in 24-hour
  mode itself, so the chip it talks to is always read in 24-hour form (the model switches the read
  format only if a guest program writes bit 6, as the real chip does).
- Weekday (register 3): not read by the firmware from the chip. It is written: 1 to 7, Sunday as 7,
  which is the numbering the model starts with, so the firmware's writes never change it.
- Date: tens from bits 5:4 (read as two bits), units from bits 3:0.
- Month: bit 4 means ten, and the low nibble is the units. **The century bit (bit 7) is ignored**
  on read (it is masked out by the `sbfx` of bit 4 and the nibble mask) and **written as 0**. For 2000
  to 2099 the chip never sets it, so a model can leave it at 0.
- Year: tens from bits 7:4, units from bits 3:0, plus 2000 on the screen.

The sync function (0x08069FC0) first looks at the options byte at 0x200118F8 + 0xAD (the DS3231 flag;
read from the disassembly, and switching the option on and off behaves as that predicts), and does
nothing if it is clear. Then it reads the seven bytes, converts them as above, and calls the HAL's
set-time (0x08007C46) and set-date (0x08007D0A) on the STM32 RTC, printing `RTC hardware error`
(0x080E68EC) if either fails. So the STM32 RTC is overwritten from the chip at each sync, and in
between it runs on emulated time. `TIME$` and `DATE$` reads go to the chip every time while the
option is on, unless `OPTION MILLISECONDS ON` (options byte 0x21), when the STM32 clock is used
(manual).

When it syncs (observed, count of 7-byte reads in the scratch runs, all with the prompt reached by
900 million instructions):

| Run | 7-byte reads |
|---|---|
| boot, no command | 4, all between the banner and the prompt |
| boot, then `PRINT 1` | 6 |
| boot, then three `PRINT`s | 10 |
| boot, then `NEW` | 7 |

So four syncs at boot, and two for each command typed at the prompt (three for `NEW`). The three call
sites are 0x080636A6, 0x08098D9A and 0x0809E868 (the last is in the start-up code right after the
banner; I did not identify which of the others the extra sync is, and I did not try a running
program). **I saw no timed polling**: a run of 3 billion instructions (about 11 s of machine time at the
prompt after boot, idle) had exactly those four syncs and nothing after. The manual says nothing about
an hourly resync, and no call site is tied to a timer in the code I read, so I do not think it exists,
but an hour of machine time is not something I can run. That is **not established**.

Cost with a chip that answers: the prompt arrives by 414 million instructions (inside 2 million of
the banner), so a sync is small, about half a million instructions or less.

## 4. The smallest model

**Superseded.** This section is the first design, from before I built it, and is left as I wrote it
for what it says of the bus and the firmware. What was built differs: the chip's time is the host's
UTC plus an offset and not a snapshot committed at STOP (a write takes effect as each byte is
acknowledged), the registers that are written are worked out a field at a time, the weekday, the
12-hour form and the century bit are modelled, and the offset is in milliseconds. "What I built"
below is how it is.

An I2C slave on PD4 and PD5, fed by the GPIO port D, the same way `SdSlot` is fed by ports C and D.
No I2C controller registers, no interrupts.

**Bus.** Level of SDA seen by the chip: the master's, which is the ODR bit when MODER says output and
high (released, pulled up by R70) when it says input. Level of SCL: ODR bit 5 when output, else
high. Feed the slave after every `GPIOD` write that can change them (`BSRR`, `ODR` and `MODER`, as
`drive_sd` does). Read-back: `GPIOD_IDR` bit 4 is **low if the chip is pulling SDA low, else high**,
whatever `PUPDR` says (the board has external pull-ups). The current GPIO model reads an input with no
pull as low, so this override for PD4 is needed (I overrode it in the scratch copy). A system reset
puts both pins back to inputs, which is a released bus.

**Slave state machine** (all edge driven; no time in it):

- START (SDA falls with SCL high): snapshot the registers, expect an address byte.
- STOP (SDA rises with SCL high): commit the snapshot's time registers if any was written, go idle.
- SCL rising: sample SDA into the byte being received, or the master's ACK bit when sending.
- SCL falling: after the eighth bit of a received byte, drive SDA low (ACK) through the ninth clock;
  when sending, put the next bit on SDA, and release it after the eighth.
- Address 0x68 only, write or read. After a write address, the first byte is the register pointer and
  the next ones are data. The pointer increments after each byte and wraps from 0x12 to 0x00.
  After a read address, send from the pointer, incrementing, until the master does not acknowledge.
  Any other address is not acknowledged, so `I2C CHECK`-style probing behaves.
- A write address followed by a STOP with only the pointer (the firmware's read) must leave the
  pointer set for the next START: do not reset it at STOP.

**Register file**, 0x00 to 0x12:

- 0x00 to 0x06 are live: seconds, minutes, hours (24-hour, bit 6 clear), weekday (1 to 7, Monday
  1, which only has to be consistent), date, month (century bit 0), year, from `host time + offset`.
  Read from a snapshot taken at START, as the chip does, so a read that crosses a second is consistent.
- A write to any of them is applied at STOP: take the snapshot's seven registers as written, work out
  the time they mean, and set `offset = that time - host time`. That makes `TIME$ = "10:00:00"` hold
  10:00:00 and carry on counting with the host. A partial write (registers 0 to 2 only, as the
  firmware does for the time; or 3 to 6 only, for the date) works because the others come from the
  snapshot.
- 0x07 to 0x10: plain stored bytes (alarms, control 0x0E reset to 0x1C, status 0x0F with OSF set at
  power-up and cleared by a write, aging 0x10). Nothing reads them now, so storing is enough.
- 0x11 and 0x12: a fixed temperature (say 25 degrees: 0x19, 0x00). Read only.
- Do not model 12-hour mode, the century bit, alarms or the square-wave pin.

**The host clock** is a trait, so tests can fix it:

```rust
pub trait HostClock { fn now(&self) -> HostTime; } // y/m/d h:m:s in local time, no leap seconds
```

The core has no dependencies beyond `roxmltree`, so it should not read the wall clock itself. The
front end (`cmm2-emulator`, which already depends on `libc`) implements it with `time` and
`localtime_r` and hands it to the machine; tests use a fixed one.

**Local time or UTC.** The MMBasic user expects the clock to show the time on the wall, and a DS3231
holds whatever was written to it, so the host's local time is the useful value. `localtime_r` is
what the front end has. I recommend local time. (Open question 1.)

**Persistence of the offset: recommended, yes.** The real chip keeps its time on the coin cell, so a
time set with `TIME$ =` survives power off, and the emulator already keeps the STM32's calendar
(`TR` and `DR`, 8 bytes) in the `--flash` file. Without it, the clock goes back to host time at
each start, which makes `TIME$ = ` pointless across sessions and quietly differs from the board. Note
that the STM32 calendar bytes in the file do not matter once the option is on: the first sync after
the banner overwrites the STM32 RTC from the chip. I would append the offset (an `i64` number of
seconds, 8 bytes) to the file: `PERSISTENT_SIZE` becomes `USER_FLASH_SIZE + 16`, and
`load_persistent` has to keep accepting the old size (offset 0), or every existing flash file
stops loading with `PersistentSizeError`. The cost is that a time set once stays set (a session started
a week later shows the old offset, not the host time), as it does on the board. A `--rtc-reset` (or
simply deleting the file) is the way back; I would not add an option until someone asks.

## 5. Where it fits and how big

- **A shared handle, like `sd_slot()` and `usb_port()`.** `pub struct Ds3231(Rc<RefCell<Chip>>)` in a new
  `crates/cmm2-core/src/ds3231.rs`, cheap to clone, with `Ds3231::new(clock: Box<dyn HostClock>)`
  and accessors `offset()` and `set_offset()` for the front end's persistence. `MemoryMap` gets a field
  and a `ds3231()` accessor next to `sd_slot()` (`memory.rs` lines 135 and 195), `lib.rs` exports it, and
  `machine.rs` passes it to `Gpio::new` for port D at the place that does `Gpio::new(port,
  memory.sd_slot())` today (line 61). The front end replaces the default (no chip answers, or host
  time) with its `HostClock` in `main.rs`, where `sd_slot()` is taken.
- **Not a `Peripheral` or `Registers`.** The chip has no memory-mapped window. It is a wire device
  behind two GPIO pins, exactly like the SD card, so it hangs off `gpio.rs`: a field in
  `GpioRegisters`, a call in `drive_sd` (rename it, it is no longer only the SD socket) for port D,
  and the PD4 case in `pin_level`.
- **Reset.** The chip is not reset by an MCU reset. The GPIO reset already runs `drive_sd`, which then
  presents a released bus; the slave goes idle on it.
- **Persistence.** `Cpu::persistent` and `load_persistent` in `cpu.rs` (the code at lines 352 to 380) add the
  offset, as above. The tests that check the saved size (one is in `cpu.rs`) need updating.
- **Size.** About 250 lines of model (bus, state machine, register file, calendar arithmetic: my
  throwaway one is 152 lines without tests and without the 12-hour or the stored registers), 30 lines
  in `gpio.rs`, 15 in `memory.rs`, `machine.rs` and `lib.rs`, 40 in the front end (the host clock and the
  persisted offset), 30 in `cpu.rs`. Tests are another 300 lines or so. Call it 650 lines in all, a day's
  work, with the calendar arithmetic (days from civil date and back) written and tested on its own
  rather than copied from the scratch.

**Testing it deterministically.**

- Unit tests of the slave alone, with a `FixedClock`. Drive it with a helper that does what the
  firmware's routines do (set a level, then the other; a byte is eight SCL pulses and a ninth for the
  acknowledge), as the SD tests do in `gpio.rs`. Cases: a write to 0x68 is acknowledged and one to
  0x69 is not; read of registers 0 to 6 at a fixed time (including a date in December, for the `sbfx`
  case above, the last day of the month and the year, and a leap day); the firmware's own shape (write
  pointer, STOP, START, read address, 7 bytes, last not acknowledged); pointer wrap at 0x12; a write of
  registers 0 to 2 sets the offset and a later read, with the fixed clock advanced, counts on from the
  value written; the same for 3 to 6, and for a write that crosses midnight; a read that crosses a
  second boundary between START and STOP gives the snapshot; STOP with only a pointer keeps the pointer.
- Through the real GPIO port (`GpioRegisters` for port D, with `MODER`, `BSRR` and `IDR`): SDA reads
  high with nothing driving, low in the acknowledge slot, and high again after; the override for PD4
  that ignores `PUPDR`; a reset releases the bus.
- Persistence: `persistent()` then `load_persistent()` into a fresh machine keeps the offset; an old
  sized file still loads, with offset 0.
- Real firmware (an ignored test, like the one that takes `CMM2_FIRMWARE`): a flash file with the option
  on and a fixed clock, `--on "> " "?TIME$:?DATE$\r"`, the output compared against the fixed time; then
  `TIME$="10:00:00"` and `?TIME$`, and the same with `DATE$`. The runs take 25 seconds each in a release
  build, as the prompt is 412 million instructions away; a flash file with the option already on is
  made once (the first boot, the keyboard and the clock, then `OPTION DS3231 ON`, then the reset) and
  kept as a fixture, or built by the test. A CLI option to start the chip at a given time
  (`--rtc-time "2026-11-14 12:00:00"`, which also freezes it) would let `cli.rs` run the same checks
  and let scripts that print `TIME$` be repeatable; I recommend it. Without it, any script that
  prints the time is different each run.

## What I could not establish

- Whether anything resyncs on a timer (an hour of machine time was not run), and which of the three
  call sites is the second sync after each command. I did not try a running program, so I do not know
  whether program start or end syncs.
- The behaviour of the real chip on this board when it does not answer. The emulator's no-chip
  behaviour above is what the firmware does against a wire that stays high; the real board with the
  option on and no chip fitted should do the same, but I have not seen it.
- Timing: the firmware's per-edge delays are 1 microsecond calls. The model is edge driven and has no
  need of them; I did not check that the real chip would tolerate that speed (it does: the I2C
  fast-mode limit is 400 kHz and this loop is far slower, as each edge is a function call, but I did not
  measure the period).
- The meaning of the options byte at 0x200118F8 + 0xAD beyond "it is the DS3231 flag" (read from the
  code and consistent with every run, not named in any source I have).
- Whether the real board's DS3231 stays in 24-hour mode across `TIME$` writes (the firmware writes
  hours with bit 6 clear, which sets the chip to 24-hour; I would expect so).
- The ODR/IDR behaviour of the real STM32 pins when SDA is switched between output and input mid-byte
  was not read from the board. The model assumes ordinary open-bus behaviour.

## Decisions

These are my answers to the open questions the spike left, as settled on the issue:

1. The host clock is the PC's local time (`localtime_r`, the system's time zone), until the guest
   sets the time. After that the chip is a clock of its own, as the real one is: it counts seconds
   evenly and knows nothing of daylight saving or time zones.
2. What the chip keeps is saved in the `--flash` file, so a time or date set with `TIME$ =` or
   `DATE$ =` goes on counting while the emulator is closed, as it does on the battery. Deleting the
   file is the way back to the host's time. There is no option for it.
3. There is no `--rtc-time` option and no frozen time. Tests inject a fixed clock in code.
4. The chip is always fitted, so that `OPTION DS3231 ON` never gives the slow boot.
5. The SBFX fix is in the tree, and the tests below cover October to December.

## What I built

The model is `crates/cmm2-core/src/ds3231.rs`, with as many lines again of tests, wired in as the
investigation above says.

- **The chip.** An I2C slave that is only fed levels of SDA and SCL and is told nothing about
  time, so it needs no delays. START and STOP are SDA moving while SCL is high, a bit is sampled
  on a rising SCL, and the chip changes what it drives on a falling one (the acknowledge after the
  eighth bit, and the next bit of a read). Address 0x68, read or write; anything else is not
  acknowledged and the chip stays out of that transaction. The register pointer wraps from 0x12 to 0
  and keeps its place across a STOP, which the firmware's read relies on. A pointer written past
  0x12 is taken modulo 0x13; the datasheet does not say and nothing writes one.
- **The time.** Two states. Until the guest writes a time register ("never set") the chip shows the
  host's local time, so it follows a daylight saving change or a new time zone as the PC's clock
  does. The first write to registers 0 to 6 makes it a clock of its own: its time is the host's UTC
  time plus an offset in milliseconds, so it counts evenly from then on. A time set at 00:59:59
  as the clocks go forward reads 10:00:02 two seconds after being set to 10:00:00, not 11:00:02, and
  a move to another zone does not touch it. The offset includes the zone the host was in when the
  time was set.
- **Reading.** Registers 0x00 to 0x06 are taken from that time when a transaction starts, and again
  when the pointer wraps from 0x12 to 0x00, as the real chip's buffer is, so a read that runs over a
  second boundary is consistent. The hours are 24-hour until the chip is written in 12-hour form
  (see below). The weekday is the chip's own count (see below), 1 to 7. A chip that has not been
  told one shows the weekday of the day it is on, Monday as 1 and Sunday as 7, and a chip that has
  been set counts on from the weekday it has by days: past 28 February 2100 that is the chip's day
  count, so it is not the weekday the Gregorian calendar gives the date. The year register has two
  digits; the century bit (bit 7 of the month) is set while the year is in an odd hundred counted
  from 2000, so it toggles when the year rolls over from 99 to 00, as on the chip. The date
  arithmetic is right well beyond 2199 (in the host's calendar, or the chip's once set), and the
  firmware ignores the bit.
- **Writing.** Each byte takes effect as the chip acknowledges it, so a transaction cut short (a
  reset, which drops it) keeps what the chip had taken. A write to one field of the time changes
  only that field: the others go on counting, so a second that passes between the bytes is not
  lost, and a `DATE$ =` (registers 3 to 6) leaves the time of day alone. Only a write to the
  seconds register restarts the second, as on the real chip (its countdown chain is reset), so the
  new second begins when it is written. The date registers are held as they were written for the
  rest of the transaction, because a date goes in a register at a time and the 30th does not fit
  the February the chip is in until the month has gone in after it. Fields out of range are
  clamped (month 13, hour 25, a year of 0xFF is 99).

  These follow the datasheet where it says what the chip does. **The weekday** is a register the
  chip counts itself: it is the value that was written (the datasheet leaves what the numbers mean
  to the user, so long as they go in order), it goes on a day at midnight and 7 goes round to 1, and
  it has nothing to do with the date, so writing the date or the time leaves it as it was. Only
  three bits are taken, and 0, which the datasheet does not have, is 1. A write of the weekday does
  not set the time. (It is kept as how many days it is from the weekday of the day the chip is on,
  which is what is saved. On a chip never set the date is the host's local one, so a change of time
  zone that takes the local date back across midnight takes the weekday back with it, as it takes
  the date, and that is as it should be for a chip that follows the host's local time. Once the time
  is set the chip counts evenly and the zone does not matter.) A write that is clamped to the 200
  years leaves the weekday as it was, counted on from the day the chip ends up on.

  **The hours** are in 12-hour form, as the chip switches its read format with bit 6 of what was
  written: bit 5 is PM, the hour is 1 to 12 and midnight is 12 AM, and it is read back so until
  hours are written in 24-hour form again. The firmware always writes bit 6 clear.

  **The century bit** is written with the month: 0 is 2000 to 2099 and 1 is 2100 to 2199, the years
  the chip has. This is my reading of the datasheet, which says only that the bit toggles when the
  year register rolls over from 99 to 00, and not its words; it is how the bit comes out for those
  years, and what the usual drivers take it to mean. So the month written back as it was read
  changes nothing in any of those years, and a `DATE$ =` in the 2100s, which writes bit 0, is a date
  in the 2000s, as it is on the chip. A year written with no month stays in the hundred years the
  chip is in. For a time outside 2000 to 2199, which only the host's clock or an offset from outside
  can give, the bit is read as the odd or even hundred counted from 2000 and the two digits of the
  year are the rest: it shows what the bit and the two digits show, and the chip is not asked to be
  right there.

  **The leap rule** is the chip's: every year divisible by 4 is a leap year, 2100 as well, where the
  Gregorian calendar the host has is not. A chip that has been set counts in that calendar, so it
  goes from 28 February 2100 to 29 February and then to 1 March, a written 29/02/00 in the 2100s is
  a date, and other months check against the same rule. It is the Gregorian calendar up to 28
  February 2100. A chip that has never been set shows the host's own, real date, with no 29 February
  2100, and takes the chip's rule from its first write.

  **Left undefined by the datasheet**, with a behaviour of mine that is the same every time: a month
  written that the day of the month does not fit (31 January, then month 2) is the last day of the
  month it is (28 February), so the chip always shows a date there is; the day that was written is
  kept for the rest of that transaction only, so that a date written in the order day, month goes in
  whole. A day of 0 is 1. A write that would put the chip more than 200 years from the host's clock
  puts it at 200 years (the most a saved offset may be, so that what it saves can be loaded), the
  same as the 200 years it may be given from outside.
- **The other registers.** 0x07 to 0x10 are plain stored bytes (control starts at 0x1C). Status
  (0x0F) starts at 0x88 as a new chip does (OSF, the oscillator has stopped, and EN32kHz): the flags
  OSF, A2F and A1F can only be cleared by a write (a 1 leaves them), BSY (bit 2) is the chip's own
  and never set, and EN32kHz is a switch. 0x11 and 0x12 are a fixed 25 degrees that a write does not
  change. The firmware never reads or writes any of them, so nothing changes at boot.
- **The host clock.** `HostClock::now` gives a `HostNow`: UTC in milliseconds, which runs evenly,
  and the wall-clock `HostTime` of the zone the host is in, which does not. The core has no clock of
  its own. `FixedClock` stands still until `advance` (or `advance_millis`) is called, and `set_zone`
  puts its wall clock ahead of UTC, as a daylight saving change does. It is what the tests use and
  what the chip has until a front end hands in another (2000-01-01 00:00:00, the STM32 RTC's reset
  time). `cmm2-emulator` has `LocalClock` (`local_clock.rs`, `clock_gettime` and
  `libc::localtime_r`), and `build_machine` sets it on the memory map's chip, so every run has the
  chip fitted. glibc reads the time zone once, at the first call, so a zone changed while the
  emulator runs is not seen until the next run; a daylight saving change is, as the zone's rules
  are applied to each time. If the host's clock is stepped (by NTP, or by hand) while the emulator
  runs, a chip never set follows it, and a chip that was set follows the step in UTC; a real chip
  would not see the host's steps at all. That only matters while the emulator is running.
- **The shared handle.** `Ds3231Slot`, like `SdSlot`: `MemoryMap::ds3231()` gives a clone, and
  `Gpio::new` takes one. The front end and the tests can read the time the chip shows (`time`),
  the offset (`offset_millis`, `None` while never set) and set it (`set_offset_millis`, which refuses
  more than 200 years from the host's clock), change the clock (`set_host_clock`), and save and
  load its state (`saved`, `load`, `load_new`, `load_v1`).
- **The wiring.** In `gpio.rs`, port D passes what it drives on PD4 and PD5 to the chip after every
  write that can change them (`MODER`, `ODR`, `BSRR`), the way it passes the SD socket's wires
  (`drive_sd` became `drive_wires`). A pin that is not an output is released, which the board's
  10K pull-ups hold high. PD4 and PD5 read high as inputs whatever `PUPDR` says, and PD4 reads low
  only while the chip pulls SDA low. A push-pull output reads its own level, as before. A system
  reset puts the pins back to inputs, which drops a transaction in progress. The registers and the
  offset stay, as the chip is not reset.
- **Persistence.** The `--flash` file is the user flash and the STM32's TR and DR as before, 917,512
  bytes, and a DS3231 that is as it was made (never set, status still 0x88, no weekday written,
  hours 24-hour) adds nothing, so the file stays that size and a build from before the chip was
  modelled can still read it. Once the guest has set the time, or cleared a status flag, or written
  a weekday or the 12-hour form, ten bytes follow (917,522 in all): the offset in milliseconds (an
  i64, little-endian), a byte whose bit 0 says the time has been set, bits 1 to 3 are the weekday
  count and bit 4 the 12-hour form (an older file has them 0, which is the weekday the date says and
  24-hour), and the status register. The battery keeps the status flags, so they are kept with it. A
  file of 917,520 bytes, which the first version with the chip wrote (the offset in seconds from the
  host's *local* time), still loads: it is turned into the same time as an offset from UTC using the
  host's zone at the load, and 0 is a chip never set. That version counted in Gregorian days, so the
  date it showed at the load is converted to the chip's own calendar exactly (the same up to 28
  February 2100). It is written back in the new form. An offset of more than 200 years is refused
  when the file is loaded, with a message, and nothing of the file is loaded (that is, nothing of
  the chip: the check of an old file's offset with the zone added is made before anything is reset).
  The ten bytes of the build with the weekday and the 12-hour form (e32008d), before the chip had
  its own calendar, were also worked out in Gregorian days, and the file cannot tell them from this
  build's: it has the same size and flags. They are read in the chip's calendar, which is the same
  up to 28 February 2100 and one day later after it, so a chip that build left set to a date past 28
  February 2100 loads one day early. Nothing before that date is affected, nor anything on the
  board, which is a chip set in this century; it is left as it is rather than guessed at. The error
  for a file of any other size names the three sizes that load.

### Tests

- Unit tests of the slave alone with a `FixedClock` (`ds3231.rs`): the calendar arithmetic (known
  days, leap days, round trips, through 2199), the chip's own calendar (the host's up to 28 February
  2100, the extra 29 February in 2100 and 2200 and not in 2400, and round trips of its days), BCD of
  a date in each of September to December (the SBFX case: month 10 to 12 needs the tens bit), Sunday
  and Monday, the last day of a leap February and of a year, the address (0x68 acknowledged, others
  not, and the chip then quiet), START and STOP against data setup, a read that ends on a NACK, the
  chip letting go of SDA for the master's acknowledge, pointer wrap at 0x12 and the time taken again
  at the wrap, a pointer surviving a STOP, a snapshot that stays consistent across a second, `TIME$
  =` and `DATE$ =` shaped writes then reads counting on from the host clock, a second passing
  between the bytes of a write, the 30th written before its month, the second restarting only for
  the seconds register (with millisecond clocks), a write cut short, out of range values and an
  invalid year, 12-hour hours and reading them back in that form, the weekday as written and counted
  on at midnight and left alone by the date and the time, a month the day does not fit, the century
  bit written, read and written back for 2000 to 2199 and the years 1999 and 2100, a set chip
  counting 28 February 2100 to 29 February and 1 March with a written 29/02/00 valid, a DATE$ in the
  2000s after the chip rolled over to the 2100s (31/12/2099 to 01/01/00 and then 01/01/30), the cap
  on a write and a clamped write leaving the weekday alone, the stored registers, the status flags
  and BSY, the read-only temperature, a chip never set following a clock change and a chip that was
  set counting evenly through one and through a change of zone, the saved state and loading it, an
  offset out of range, and the offset of the first version, which is refused before anything is
  reset.
- Through the GPIO port (`gpio.rs`): a read and a write clocked out of PD4 and PD5 with BSRR and
  MODER as the firmware does it, SDA low only in the acknowledge slot, idle high whatever `PUPDR`
  says, a released SCL as a high clock, port C's pins 4 and 5 not reaching the chip, and a reset
  during an acknowledge.
- Persistence (`cpu.rs`, `main.rs`): the file stays the old size for a chip never set, the longer
  form with a set time and with only the status cleared, loading all three sizes, a refused offset
  leaving everything alone, the wrong sizes and the message naming the right ones, and through the
  `--flash` file in `main.rs`. A front end test that the host clock is attached.
- The real firmware (ignored, `CMM2_FIRMWARE`, in release, `real_firmware.rs`), with a fixed host
  clock of Thursday 2026-12-24 13:45:56: with `OPTION DS3231 ON` the reboot reaches the prompt at
  413.0 million instructions (412 million with the option off, against the 1.3 billion that nothing
  answering took); `TIME$` and `DATE$` print `13:45:56` and `24-12-2026`; `TIME$ = "10:20:30"`
  reads back at once, and 90 seconds of host time later reads `10:22:00`; `DATE$ = "05/11/27"` shows
  and leaves the time alone; a `persistent` saved after `TIME$ =` and `DATE$ =` loaded into a
  new machine an hour of host time later shows the time an hour on; and a chip never set follows
  the host's zone change to 14:45:56 and then, once set to 10:00:00, counts on to 10:00:02 through
  another. Each takes about a minute and the first of them makes the saved state with the option
  on, which the others share.

I checked the tests by mutation (a copy of the tree, one change at a time: the bit sampled, START
and STOP swapped, the bit order, the pointer wrap, the BCD masks, the weekday, the pull-up, the SDA
and SCL wiring, the reset, the local-or-UTC choice, the sub-second restart, the date registers held
for the transaction, the century, the 12-hour form, the status rules, the saved state and its
sizes). All were caught, except two changes that turned out not to be needed and were removed. The
weekday, the 12-hour form, the century write, the cap on a write, the all-or-nothing load of an old
offset and the saved flags were checked the same way afterwards, change by change, and all were caught.

What I did not build: the alarms, the square-wave pin and the 32 kHz output (the pins are not wired
on the board) and the bus timeout. The firmware uses none of them.
